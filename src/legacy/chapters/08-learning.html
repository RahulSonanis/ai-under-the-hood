How does a model learn?

Short answer

It starts with random numbers and makes terrible guesses. After each batch of text, calculus works out which direction to nudge every number to make the guesses a little less wrong. Repeat millions of times.

Roll downhill on a loss landscape

Simulation · gradient descent

The map is the model's error for every setting of two numbers: dark means low error. Click anywhere (or focus the map and use the arrow keys) to drop a ball, then press Run. It can only feel the slope under it. Try a tiny step size, a huge one, and turning on momentum.

A real model has billions of numbers, not two, so its landscape can't be drawn, but the rule is identical: compute the slope (gradient) and step against it. This surface is a made-up function with two valleys; modern optimisers such as AdamW add momentum and per-parameter step sizes.

A genuine network: one input, one hidden layer of tanh neurons, one output, trained with mean-squared error, hand-written backpropagation and the Adam optimiser58. An LLM is the same idea scaled up: billions of weights, cross-entropy on next tokens instead of squared error. Small spikes in the loss curve are normal with Adam at higher learning rates.

Gradient descent

You're on a foggy hill and want to reach the bottom. You can't see far, but you can feel which way the ground slopes. So you take a small step downhill, feel again, and repeat.

The "hill" is the model's error. The "ground" is all its numbers. The slope tells each number whether to go up or down to make the error smaller. Steps that are too small take forever; steps that are too big overshoot and bounce around or fly off.

LLMs use mini-batch stochastic gradient descent: each step uses a batch of a few million tokens, so the slope is a noisy estimate. The most widely used optimiser is AdamW68, a variant of Adam58 that keeps running averages of the gradient and its square for every parameter. Alternatives are appearing: Kimi K2 was trained with MuonClip, a variant of the Muon optimiser69. The learning rate follows a schedule: a short warm-up, then a decay, either cosine or warmup–stable–decay (WSD), which SmolLM3 and Kimi K2 used1.

θ ← θ − η · ∇_θ L(θ) (plain SGD)
m ← β₁m + (1−β₁)g ; v ← β₂v + (1−β₂)g² ; θ ← θ − η·( m̂ / (√v̂ + ε) + λθ )

(AdamW, with bias-corrected m̂, v̂ and decoupled weight decay λ.) Typical LLM settings: β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.95, peak η around 10⁻⁴ to 10⁻³, gradient-norm clipping at 1.01.

Backpropagation

After a wrong guess, the blame is passed backwards through the network, so every part learns how much it contributed to the mistake.

The network is a long chain of simple steps. Working backwards from the error, each step can calculate how much its own numbers affected the result. One backward pass gives the slope for every single number at once.

Frameworks such as PyTorch and JAX record the forward computation and apply the chain rule automatically. The backward pass costs about twice the forward pass, which is where "training ≈ 6 FLOPs per parameter per token" comes from: 2 forward plus 4 backward7. It also needs the forward activations kept in memory, a major reason training needs far more memory than serving (chapter 10).

For a layer y = f(Wx), ∂L/∂W = (∂L/∂y ⊙ f′(Wx)) xᵀ and ∂L/∂x = Wᵀ(∂L/∂y ⊙ f′(Wx)). Reverse-mode differentiation computes the full gradient ∇_θL in time proportional to one forward evaluation, independent of the number of parameters.

When training goes wrong

Sometimes the ball suddenly jumps up the hill. Teams watch for this all the time.

Large runs can suffer loss spikes, sudden jumps in error. Some recover on their own; others need the team to roll back to an earlier save and change something, such as lowering the learning rate or skipping bad data.

Stability tricks include gradient clipping, warm-up, removing weight decay from embeddings, z-loss on the output logits and QK-norm in attention1. DeepSeek reported that DeepSeek-V3's run had no irrecoverable loss spikes and no rollbacks10, notable enough to state. Hugging Face restarted SmolLM3's run after a subtle infrastructure bug surfaced around 1T tokens1.

Check yourself

The loss bounces up and down and never settles. What's the most likely fix?

Steps that are too big overshoot the valley. Smaller steps, or a schedule that decays them, let it settle.

Why does training cost about three times as much compute per token as a forward pass alone?

2 FLOPs per parameter forward plus about 4 backward gives the familiar 6·N per token.
