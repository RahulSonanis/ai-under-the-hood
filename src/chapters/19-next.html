After the course · Build it yourself

What should you build to make it stick?

The fastest way to really understand this is to build a small version of each part yourself and measure it. Every project below fits on a laptop or a GPU rented for an hour or two, and each one is tied to the chapters it brings to life.

Each project ends with "Done when you can show": the evidence to produce. There is no right number to hit. Your numbers will differ from the course's, and explaining why is part of the work.

Part 1 · How a model answers

Look at the model from the outside, then build a tiny one.

Chapter 1Chapter 9

Time a reply, token by token

Call any model API with streaming switched on and record the moment each token arrives. Do it for a one-line question and for a long pasted document.

Done when you can show

A timeline for each request with the time to the first token and the gaps between tokens, plus one sentence on why the long document changed the first number more than the gaps.

Start with: server-sent events55, an API's message docs70
Chapter 2

Train a tokenizer

Train a small byte-pair-encoding tokenizer (the method that merges the most common pairs of pieces) on a few megabytes of text, then measure how many characters each token covers in different languages.

Done when you can show

Characters per token for at least three languages, how a few long or rare words were split, and a sentence on why the languages differ.

Start with: the original BPE paper4
Chapter 3Chapter 4Chapter 13

Train a tiny GPT

Train a small character-level transformer on a short book on your laptop or one cloud GPU. Watch the loss (the model's average surprise) fall and the samples improve.

Done when you can show

Your settings and training time, the training and held-out loss curves, and samples from early, middle and late in training, with your notes on what changed as the loss fell.

Start with: nanoGPT46, The Illustrated Transformer47
Chapter 3Chapter 5

Play with sampling and voting

Using your tiny model or a small open one, generate from one prompt at several temperatures and top-p settings. Then, on a set of short maths questions, sample several answers per question and take a majority vote.

Done when you can show

Five samples per setting side by side with notes on where text turned dull or incoherent, and a chart of accuracy against the number of votes (1, 3, 5, 9) on at least 30 questions, with the tokens each point cost.

Start with: nucleus sampling39, self-consistency503

Part 2 · Serving millions

Run a model as a service and watch where the time, memory and money go.

Chapter 7Chapter 8Chapter 9

Serve a model and load it

Serve a small open model with vLLM, an open-source serving engine, on one rented GPU. Send it more and more requests at once and measure what happens.

Done when you can show

A chart of time to first token and total tokens per second at each concurrency level, with the model, GPU and prompt lengths, and where you think it saturates and why (memory for notes, or compute).

Start with: vLLM54, PagedAttention34
Chapter 10

Roll out a new version safely

Put two versions of your served model behind a small router that sends a slice of traffic to the new one. Write an automatic check that grades a sample of replies, plant a fault in the new version, and roll it out step by step.

Done when you can show

A log of each rollout stage with traffic share, error rate and check results, and the point where your check stopped the rollout (or an honest note on why it didn't).

Start with: Canarying releases1001
Chapter 11

Keep an infrastructure log

Each month, pick one new AI cluster announcement and note its chips, power, network and cooling. Estimate its power draw yourself from the chip count.

Done when you can show

A dated table of at least three clusters with sources, and your power estimate next to the announced figure with the assumptions you used.

Start with: Epoch AI's data48, Google's efficiency figures45

Part 3 · Where the model comes from

Clean data, train at a few sizes, spread training over two GPUs, then turn a predictor into an assistant.

Chapter 12

Clean a slice of the web

Take a sample of web text, filter out obvious junk, and find near-duplicates with MinHash, a quick way to estimate how much two documents overlap.

Done when you can show

How many documents each filter removed, how many near-duplicates you found at your threshold, three pairs it caught and one it missed or wrongly merged.

Start with: FineWeb3, Broder's resemblance paper1202
Chapter 14

Scaling on a budget

Fix a small compute budget and train three model sizes with it: big model on few tokens, small model on many, and one in between.

Done when you can show

Each model's size, tokens, compute and held-out loss, plotted as your own small curve, with your reading of the trend, even if noise hides it or your sizes miss the best point.

Start with: Chinchilla8, the Smol Training Playbook1
Chapter 14

Split training over two GPUs

Rent two GPUs for an hour. Run the same training job twice: once with a full copy of the model on each GPU, once with the model's state split between them (FSDP).

Done when you can show

A table of peak memory per GPU and time per training step for both setups, with the settings, and an explanation of the difference.

Start with: the Ultra-Scale Playbook2, ZeRO11
Chapter 15

Make a small assistant

Fine-tune a small open model on example conversations, then on a dataset of preferred and rejected answers. Compare the three versions.

Done when you can show

The same ten prompts answered by the base, example-tuned and preference-tuned models side by side, with the datasets and settings, and your notes on what changed and what got worse.

Start with: Hugging Face TRL49, the DPO paper18

Part 4 · Around the model

Build the loop that turns a model into an agent, then try to break it.

Chapter 16Chapter 17

Write your own agent loop

In about 200 lines, write a loop that lets a model call two tools, "read a file" and "run the tests", on a small project with a planted bug. Add a token budget and a step that summarises old context when it grows too long.

Done when you can show

A log of every model call and tool call, the tokens used against your budget, and whether the tests passed in the end.

Start with: Building effective agents40, context engineering41, MCP42
Chapter 17

Attack your own agent

Hide an instruction inside one of the files your agent reads, for example a comment asking it to delete the tests. Then add one defence, such as asking for confirmation before risky tools run.

Done when you can show

The agent's log with and without the defence, how often the hidden instruction steered it over at least ten runs, and what your defence did and did not stop.

Start with: your loop from the project before, and chapter 17's simulation

Where to go next: a learning path

This course gives you the map. These are some of the best places to go deeper, roughly from beginner to expert.

  1. Beginner
    3Blue1Brown: Neural networks and transformers

    Beautiful visual intuition for neurons, gradient descent and attention.

  2. Beginner
    Andrej Karpathy: Intro to Large Language Models

    A one-hour talk on what language models are and how they're made.

  3. Intermediate
    Jay Alammar: The Illustrated Transformer

    The classic diagram-by-diagram walk through a transformer.

  4. Intermediate
    Hugging Face LLM Course

    Hands-on with tokenizers, models and fine-tuning in code.

  5. Engineer
    Andrej Karpathy: Neural Networks, Zero to Hero

    Build backpropagation, a tokenizer and a GPT from scratch, line by line.

  6. Engineer
    Sebastian Raschka: Build a Large Language Model (From Scratch)

    A book-length build of a GPT-style model, including fine-tuning.

  7. Engineer
    Smol Training Playbook and Ultra-Scale Playbook

    How real models are trained, and how training is spread across thousands of GPUs.

  8. Expert
    Stanford CS336: Language Modeling from Scratch

    A full university course: data, architectures, systems, scaling and alignment.

  9. Expert
    Lilian Weng's blog

    Deep, well-referenced surveys of attention, training from feedback, hallucination and more.

How to keep learning

Keep a short notebook per chapter: the one idea that matters, one number you measured yourself, and one question you couldn't answer yet. Revisit the datacenter chapter every few months, because that part of the field changes fastest. When a lab publishes a technical report or a postmortem, read it against these chapters: most of it will map onto something you've already played with.
