Chapter 14 · Where the model comes from

How do thousands of chips train one model?

A large model is trained on thousands of GPUs at once, for weeks, as one job. With that many chips, something breaks every few hours, and every break stops all of them. This chapter hands you the plan: how big, how much data, how to split it, and how often to save.

About 16 minutes · one simulation · you'll learn how teams size a model for their compute, why a model is split across many GPUs, and how training survives constant failures.

The work. In training, every parameter (one of the model's adjustable numbers) does a few calculations for every token it reads. The total is about 6 × parameters × tokens. How it works scaling laws, parallelism and checkpoints

Scaling laws. Researchers found that loss (how surprised the model is by the next token) falls smoothly and predictably as models and data grow, so a team can test small models and predict a big one7. DeepMind's Chinchilla study showed that for a fixed budget, parameters and tokens should grow together, around 20 tokens per parameter8. Panel 1 uses Epoch AI's re-fit of that law50. In practice teams often train small models far past that point, because a smaller model is cheaper to run for millions of users: Meta's Llama 3 8B read about 15 trillion tokens, close to 1,900 per parameter9.

Memory. With mixed-precision training, each parameter has a 2-byte weight and a 2-byte gradient (its "which way to nudge"), plus 12 bytes the optimiser keeps: a precise copy of the weight and two running averages11. That's the 16 bytes. Activations, the in-between results of each step, need more on top (10 GB in the simulation, illustrative). Real systems also shard the optimiser's notes across the data-parallel copies (ZeRO, FSDP), which the simulation leaves out.

Three ways to split. Data parallel: every copy reads different text, and the copies average their gradients after each step with an all-reduce. In the common ring version each GPU passes chunks to its neighbour, so each one sends about twice the gradient size whatever the number of GPUs2. Tensor parallel: each matrix multiplication is cut across GPUs, which swap partial results inside every layer, so it stays within one server's very fast links12. Pipeline parallel: groups of layers sit on different servers and small batches flow through like an assembly line; the line is partly empty at the start and end of each step13. Meta trained its 405-billion-parameter Llama 3 model with 8-way tensor, 16-stage pipeline and 128-way data parallelism on 16,384 GPUs9.

Failures and checkpoints. Meta's run saw 419 unexpected interruptions in 54 days; about 78% were confirmed or suspected hardware, mostly GPUs and their memory. Automation handled almost all of them and useful training time stayed above 90%959. A checkpoint writes the whole training state to storage; teams copy it to CPU memory first so the GPUs pause only briefly15. Save and restart times in the simulation (1 and 6 minutes) are illustrative.

The maths how long a run takes
In words

Total work is 6 × parameters × tokens. Divide by how fast the cluster does useful maths: the number of GPUs, times each GPU's top speed, times the share of that speed actually used (pipeline waiting lowers it), times the share of time whose work is kept (saves and failures lower it).

T = 6ND / (G · F · u · k)  ·  k ≈ 1 − δ/τ − (τ/2 + R)/M  ·  τ* ≈ √(2δM)

T time to finish · N parameters · D training tokens · G GPUs (16,384) · F one GPU's peak speed (989 trillion operations per second) · u share of peak used: 50% here, times (1 − bubble), where the bubble is (p − 1)/(m + p − 1) for p pipeline stages and m small batches (64) · k share of time kept · δ time to save (1 min) · τ time between saves · R time to restart (6 min) · M average time between failures (about 3 hours for 16,384 GPUs). Each failure loses on average half a save interval plus the restart. The best interval τ* balances the two costs (Young's rule, refined by Daly43): about 19 minutes here.

Check yourself

A team doubles its cluster to 32,768 GPUs of the same kind. What happens to the time between failures that stop the job?

Each GPU fails at the same rate, so twice as many GPUs means twice as many failures: half the time between them. The best save interval gets shorter too.

A 70-billion-parameter model needs about 1.1 TB of memory to train. Why doesn't adding more data-parallel copies help it fit?

Data parallelism multiplies copies to read more text at once. To fit, each copy must be cut into pieces, or its learning notes sharded across copies.
