Why are AI companies building power plants?

In 2024, Microsoft, which runs some of the world's largest datacenters, agreed to buy 20 years of electricity from a nuclear reactor being restarted at Three Mile Island in Pennsylvania1104. That reactor makes about 835 MW, as much as roughly 680,000 American homes use1101. This chapter gives you a power budget and an empty site, and asks you to build an AI cluster.

What's going on

Every chip is a heater. An AI chip draws about 1 kW. With its share of the processors, memory and network around it, each chip in your cluster needs about 1.65 kW60. Your 0 chips draw 0 MW, and every bit of that becomes heat the building must carry away.

Cooling sets how tightly chips can be packed. Fans blowing cold air can cool an ordinary rack: the most common ones hold 4–6 kW of equipment1103. The newest AI racks hold 72 chips and about 120 kW, which only works with liquid piped straight onto the chips60. Packing matters because chips that work together need to sit close, joined by short, fast cables. With air, your halls fill up long before your power runs out.

The building takes its share. PUE (power usage effectiveness) is the site's total power divided by the power that reaches the computers. At your PUE of 1.5, 0 MW goes to chillers, fans and power conversion. The industry average in 2024 was 1.561103; Google reports about 1.09 for its own datacenters45.

So power is the limit. Your site draws 0 MW, as much as 0 homes, the size of a town. The International Energy Agency estimates that a typical AI datacenter uses as much electricity as 100,000 households, and the largest being built will use 20 times that. Connecting that much to the grid takes years: new power lines take four to eight years to build in rich countries1102. So companies build where power already is, and pay for new power plants.

How it works from chip to campus

From chip to rack. NVIDIA, the largest maker of AI chips, sells a rack called the GB200 NVL72: 72 GPUs and 36 processors wired together so tightly they act as one large accelerator22. It draws about 120 kW and requires direct-to-chip liquid cooling: metal cold plates sit on the chips, and a coolant distribution unit pumps liquid through them60. An older H100 chip is rated up to 700 W23; newer ones draw more.

Why liquid. A litre of water soaks up about 3,500 times as much heat as a litre of air for the same rise in temperature, so a thin pipe of water can do the work of a gale of air. Water use depends on the design: Microsoft's Fairwater AI datacenter in Wisconsin cools over 90% of its capacity with a closed loop of liquid that is filled once and recirculated31.

Training and serving use power differently. A training run keeps every chip busy in step, so its draw is flat until the whole job pauses at once. Meta, the company behind the open Llama models, reported that such pauses swung its datacenter's power by tens of megawatts, "stretching the limits of the power grid"9. Serving follows people's day, so the site is built for the afternoon peak and sits partly idle at night.

Campuses. Sites now come in gigawatts. One being built in Abilene, Texas, for OpenAI, the maker of ChatGPT, is designed for 1.2 GW across eight buildings and over 450,000 chips32, close to a million homes' worth of power1101. Worldwide, datacenters used about 1.5% of all electricity in 2024, and the International Energy Agency expects that to more than double by 2030, to slightly more than Japan uses today1102.

The maths from a power budget to chips, homes and days
In words

The site's power is the number of chips, times the power each chip needs with its share of the rack, times the building's overhead (PUE). Turn it around to find how many chips a power budget buys. The training time is the job's total work divided by how much work all the chips do per second.

Check yourself

Why are the newest AI racks cooled with liquid?

Water carries far more heat per litre than air, so it can cool racks many times denser than ordinary ones.

A site has a PUE of 1.2 and its computers draw 100 MW. What does the whole site draw?

PUE is the site's power divided by the computers' power, so 100 MW × 1.2 = 120 MW.
