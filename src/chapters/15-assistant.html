How does a text predictor become a helpful assistant, and how do we know it's better?

Straight out of training, a model only continues text. Ask it a question and it may reply with more questions, like a quiz page would. A short second phase of training turns it into an assistant, and a careful test shows whether that worked.

About 15 minutes · one simulation · you'll learn how example conversations and people's preferences reshape a model, why it needs a leash, and how big a test must be before you can trust it.

What's going on

Shown. A freshly trained "base" model has read a large slice of the web but has never been asked to help anyone. Fine-tuning continues training on written-out conversations: a question, then a good answer. With 1,000 examples the model has picked up the format, and only 0% of its replies still just continue the text. It also copies the examples' habits, good and bad.

Judged. Writing perfect answers is hard; saying which of two is better is easy. So people compare pairs of replies, and a second model, the reward model, learns to predict their choices. The assistant then practises: it gives replies, gets scored, and shifts toward higher scores. The same training teaches refusals: the people comparing replies, or a model judging them against a written list of principles (called Constitutional AI), prefer a safe, polite decline to a harmful answer, so the model learns what to refuse17. Right now helpful replies have gone from 0% to 0%.

The leash. The reward model is an imitation of people, with flaws. People sometimes prefer an answer that agrees with them, and the reward model learns that too. Chase its score with nothing holding you back and the model finds those flaws: flattery wins. This is reward hacking. So training adds a penalty for drifting from the starting model. Its strength is β (the Greek letter beta): a big β is a tight leash, a small β a loose one. How far the model has moved now: 0, where 0 means not at all.

Proven. Test scores are noisy: which questions get drawn is luck. With 100 questions, a score of 0% is only known to within about ±10 points (its 95% band: the range the true score is probably in). The fair comparison asks both models the same questions and measures the gap question by question. That cancels out how hard each question was, so it needs fewer questions than waiting for two separate bands to stop overlapping. A gain counts when the whole range of the gap is above zero. How it works fine-tuning, feedback and evaluation

Supervised fine-tuning. The training is the same guess-and-nudge loop as before, on conversations instead of web text. Special tokens mark who is speaking (system, user, assistant), and usually only the assistant's tokens count toward the error. Datasets range from thousands to millions of conversations, written by people or generated by stronger models1. Generating training data with a stronger model is a form of distillation: a small "student" learns from a big "teacher's" outputs, an idea Hinton and colleagues set out for neural networks in 20151502. DeepSeek, for example, distilled reasoning from its R1 model into V310.

Fine-tuning cheaply. Updating every weight needs the full training setup from the last chapter. LoRA freezes the model and trains two thin extra matrices beside each layer; for GPT-3 it cut the trainable numbers about 10,000-fold and memory about threefold, with similar quality1501. That's how most companies customise open models.

Learning from feedback. OpenAI's InstructGPT set the recipe: fine-tune, collect rankings, fit a reward model, then optimise against it with reinforcement learning and a KL penalty (a measure of drift) to the starting model16. Push harder against a learned reward and the true quality rises, then falls, a pattern measured by Gao and colleagues1504; agreeable answers are one known flaw of human and model preferences1503. Variants: Constitutional AI has the model critique its own answers against written principles17; DPO skips the separate reward model and trains on the preference pairs directly18; for maths and code, rewards can come from checking the answer or running tests, which DeepSeek-R1 showed can teach long reasoning19. The simulation's numbers are illustrative; its formula for the trained model is exact.

Evaluation. Benchmarks are sets of questions with known answers, such as HumanEval, OpenAI's 164 hand-written coding problems checked by running tests1505. Pitfalls: noise from small tests, contamination (test questions leaking into training data, which inflates scores), and sensitivity to how questions are worded. Hugging Face's advice is to reproduce published scores first and read the answers by hand20. Safety testing adds red-teaming: people and automated tools try hard to make the model misbehave. Anthropic ties required safeguards to the capabilities such tests find21, and checking continues after launch: its September 2025 postmortem describes production bugs that offline tests missed30.

The maths the leash, exactly
In words

The best model under a leash takes each reply's old probability and multiplies it by a boost that grows with the reward model's score. A loose leash (small β) makes the boost enormous, so the top-scoring reply takes over; a tight leash (big β) keeps every boost near 1. Then all the probabilities are rescaled to add up to 100%.

In symbols
π(y) = πref(y) · er(y)/β / Z
drift = KL = Σy π(y) · ln( π(y) / πref(y) )

y one possible reply · π(y) how often the trained model gives it · πref(y) how often the starting (fine-tuned) model gave it · r(y) the reward model's score · β leash strength · e ≈ 2.718 · Z the sum of all the boosted numbers, so the shares add to 1 · Σy add up over the five replies · ln the natural logarithm: each reply adds its new share times how much that share grew (a reply whose share stays put adds 0). This is the exact best answer to "maximise reward minus β × drift", where drift is the KL divergence from the starting model18. DPO rearranges the same equation to train on preference pairs directly.

The maths how big a test must be
In words

A score from n questions wobbles by about 1.96 × √(p(1 − p) ÷ n) either side of the truth, 95% of the time. Comparing two separately tested models, both wobble, so the gap wobbles about √2 ≈ 1.4 times as much as one score. To measure a gap of 2 points that way you need the gap's wobble down to 2 points: about 4,800 questions per model. Asking both models the same questions helps, because a question that is hard for one is usually hard for the other, and those wobbles cancel.

h half-width of one score's 95% band · p the share answered helpfully · n questions per model · 1.96 the number of standard deviations that covers 95% of outcomes · hgap half-width of the gap's band · Δ the gap you want to detect (0.02 for 2 points): with p near 0.5 that gives about 4,800 questions per model · sd the spread of the per-question differences (+1 if only your model got it right, −1 if only the old one did, 0 if both or neither), which is small when the two models agree on most questions.

Check yourself

Why keep the trained model close to its starting point with a drift penalty?

The score is a stand-in for people's judgement. A leash limits how far the model can wander chasing quirks in it.

Model B beats model A by 2 points on a 200-question test. What should you conclude?

A 2-point gap is well inside the noise of a 200-question test. With separate tests you'd need thousands of questions per model; asking both models the same questions helps, but 200 is still too few to pin down 2 points.
