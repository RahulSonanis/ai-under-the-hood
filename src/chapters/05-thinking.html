Chapter 5 · How a model answers

What do "thinking" models do differently?

Some assistants pause before they answer, sometimes for a minute or more. They are writing, just not to you: thousands of hidden tokens of working-out before the first visible word. This chapter lets you decide how much thinking a model gets, and shows what that buys and what it costs.

What's going on

A model writes one token at a time, and each token gets the same fixed amount of compute (the arithmetic the chip does to produce it). A hard problem can need more work than one token's worth. A thinking model (also called a reasoning model) gets around this by writing its working-out first: trying cases, checking, backing up. These thinking tokens are ordinary tokens, but the app hides them or shows a summary. Every one of them is something the model can look back at when it writes the final answer.

Spending more compute while answering is called test-time compute. On hard problems it helps a lot, then levels off (the chart's curves are illustrative, shaped like published results): once the model has worked a problem through, it stops, and the unused budget buys nothing. Another way to spend compute is to run several attempts and take a majority vote. Right answers tend to agree while wrong ones scatter, so the vote beats a single attempt.

None of it is free. Thinking tokens are written one at a time, so they add waiting before the first visible word, and providers bill them like any other output. How it works training, scaling and budgets

Writing the steps out. Researchers found early on that asking large models to show their working, by giving examples with the steps written out ("chain of thought"), made them much better at maths word problems505. Reasoning models are trained to do this by themselves, at length.

Trained by checking answers. The training method is reinforcement learning: the model tries a problem many times, and attempts that end in the right answer are rewarded. This works best on problems whose answers can be checked automatically. DeepSeek, a Chinese AI lab, rewarded its R1-Zero model only for a correct final answer (maths answers compared with the known result, code run against tests) and for putting its reasoning between <think> tags. Nobody wrote reasoning for it to copy. Over training its answers grew longer by themselves, and it began to re-check and correct its own steps19. OpenAI, the company behind ChatGPT, describes training its o1 model the same broad way and reports that accuracy keeps rising with both more training and more thinking time501.

Where extra thinking pays. A study from Google DeepMind and UC Berkeley found that the best way to spend test-time compute depends on how hard the question is. Giving easy questions little and hard ones more was more than four times as efficient as simply sampling many answers and picking the best. On easy and medium questions, a small model given extra thinking could beat a model 14 times larger, but on the hardest ones, a bigger model still won502.

Voting. "Self-consistency" samples several independent chains of reasoning and keeps the most common final answer. On a set of school maths word problems it lifted accuracy by about 18 percentage points (points on the 0 to 100% scale, from about 57% right to about 74%) over a single chain503. It multiplies the cost by the number of attempts. They can run side by side, so the wait grows much less.

Budgets in practice. Anthropic, the company that makes the Claude models, offers "extended thinking". There the developer sets budget_tokens, a target for how much to think (minimum 1,024 tokens); the model may stop well before it. Newer Claude models decide whether and how much to think themselves ("adaptive thinking"), steered by an effort setting. Either way, thinking tokens are billed as output tokens504506. Serving them is like serving any other output: each one is a full pass through the model, and each one adds notes to GPU memory, which Chapter 7 shows is the scarce resource.

Speeds and prices here. The simulated model writes 150 tokens a second, like a fast model on a lightly loaded server, to keep things moving. Chapter 1's assistant writes about 45 a second and chapter 9's busy server about 30 per conversation, so real thinking waits can be several times longer. The illustrative price, $10 per million output tokens, is a list price of the kind charged for big frontier models; list prices include the provider's margin, and chapter 9 works out what serving actually costs. The course counts costs per 1,000 replies; 1,000 ordinary replies are about a million tokens.

The maths wait and cost, worked through
In words

The wait is set by the longest attempt: its hidden tokens plus its answer, divided by how fast the model writes. The other attempts run alongside it, so they don't add to the wait. The cost counts every token written: each attempt's thinking and its own answer (that's what gets voted on), times the price of one output token.

Check yourself

Why does a thinking model take longer before its answer appears?

Thinking tokens are generated like any output, one after another, so thousands of them mean seconds or minutes of waiting.

On hard problems, a model already stops well before using its thinking budget. What can still raise accuracy?

Unused budget buys nothing. Independent attempts plus a vote spend compute in a different way, at the cost of more tokens.
