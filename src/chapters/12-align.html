How does a predictor become an assistant?

Short answer

A freshly trained "base" model just continues text. Further training on example conversations, then on feedback about which answers are better, teaches it to follow instructions, be honest about uncertainty and decline harmful requests.

Chasing reward without losing yourself

Simulation · exact optimum of the RLHF objective

Five candidate replies to a coding question. A reward model scores them; training shifts probability toward high scores, held back by a leash (β) to the original model. Loosen the leash (drag β to the left), then switch on the flawed reward model that loves flattery.

Prompt: "My Java service throws a NullPointerException on startup. Can you help?"

Small β chases reward; large β stays close to the starting model.

The optimum of "maximise reward minus β × KL distance from the reference" has the closed form π*(y) ∝ π_ref(y)·exp(r(y)/β)18; this panel computes it exactly. Rewards are illustrative. With a flawed reward and a loose leash (small β), the policy learns to flatter: a small example of reward hacking.

Supervised fine-tuning

The model has read the whole library but never had a conversation. So we show it lots of good examples: "when someone asks this, a helpful answer looks like that".

Fine-tuning continues training, but on carefully written conversations instead of raw web text. The model learns the format of turns, how to follow instructions, and what a good answer looks like. It needs far less data than pretraining.

SFT datasets range from thousands to millions of conversations written by people or generated by stronger models (distillation; DeepSeek-V3 distilled reasoning from DeepSeek-R110). Chat templates add special tokens marking system, user and assistant turns, and loss is usually computed only on the assistant's tokens. Many pipelines add a mid-training stage before SFT for long context, maths and code1.

Learning from feedback

People look at two answers and pick the better one. The model gets a treat for answers like the winners.

It's hard to write the perfect answer but easy to say which of two is better. So people compare pairs; a second model learns to predict their preferences (a reward model); then the assistant is trained to produce answers the reward model scores highly, without drifting too far from what it was.

This is RLHF, used for InstructGPT16. Variants: Constitutional AI replaces many human labels with the model critiquing its own answers against written principles17; DPO optimises preferences directly without a separate reward model18; RL with verifiable rewards scores maths and code by checking the answer or running tests, which DeepSeek-R1 showed can produce long chains of reasoning19. Reward models can be gamed, so teams use KL penalties, diverse checks and human review.

max_π E_{y∼π}[ r(x, y) ] − β · KL( π(·|x) ‖ π_ref(·|x) )

Reward models are usually fitted with the Bradley–Terry model: P(y_w ≻ y_l) = σ(r(y_w) − r(y_l)). DPO substitutes the closed-form optimum r = β log(π/π_ref) + const into that likelihood, giving a loss on preference pairs directly: −log σ(β[log π(y_w)/π_ref(y_w) − log π(y_l)/π_ref(y_l)])18.

Why keep the trained model close to the original with a KL penalty?

Reward models are imperfect. A leash limits how far the policy can wander chasing quirks in the score.
