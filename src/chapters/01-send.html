Chapter 1 · How a model answers

What happens when you press send?

Between your click and the first word of the reply, your message crosses the internet, passes three checkpoints, waits in line and is read by a chip in a datacenter. It usually all takes well under a second. This chapter lets you run that machine and find out what makes it slow.

What's going on

When you press send, your app packs your message into a request: the conversation so far, plus any hidden instructions the app adds. The request crosses the internet to a datacenter, a building full of computers. A gateway checks who you are, a quick safety check screens the message, and then it waits in line until a copy of the model has room for it.

The model then works in two very different ways. First it reads. Your text is cut into tokens, small pieces of words (the next chapter shows how), and every token goes through the model at the same time, in one pass. Your last message was – tokens, read in –. Then it writes, one token at a time: each new token is fed back in to make the next, about one every –. Every token is sent to you the moment it exists, which is why replies appear word by word.

So the wait for the first word is the trip, the line and the reading, plus any thinking. Yours was – with the service – busy. A long prompt makes the reading longer. A busy service makes the line longer, and much longer near full. A thinking model writes hundreds of hidden tokens before the first one you see. After the first word, the writing pace barely changes.

How it works requests, queues, caching, two phases

The request. Apps send an HTTPS request to the provider's API (the address programs use to talk to the service). Its body is a list of messages (hidden "system" instructions, your turns, the model's turns), the model's name, a length limit and a flag asking for the reply to be streamed. The model keeps nothing between requests, so the whole conversation is sent again every turn70. With streaming on, the reply comes back as server-sent events: one long-lived response that delivers small chunks as they are made55.

Hidden instructions. A chat app adds its own instructions, and a coding agent adds even more: rules, tool descriptions and project files. The simulation uses 2,000 and 15,000 tokens; these are illustrative, and real apps vary. Your own code sends only what you write.

The line. Each copy of the model works on a limited number of conversations at once. The simulation's line is a classic single-queue model, M/M/1: requests arrive at random, and places free up at random. Its average wait is the time to free one place (60 ms here) times ρ/(1 − ρ), where ρ is how busy the service is. At 50% busy that is 60 ms; at 95% it is over a second. Providers keep spare capacity because waits explode near full.

Caching the start. If a request begins with exactly the same tokens as a recent one, the server can reuse the notes it made while reading them instead of reading again. Routers try to send you to a copy that has your conversation's start stored. Providers bill cached text at a discount7172. Chapter 7 shows where those notes live.

Two phases. Engineers call reading prefill and writing decode, and measure them as time to first token (TTFT) and time per output token. Prefill is limited by how fast the chips calculate, since every token can be processed in parallel. Decode is limited by how fast the chips can read the model's weights from memory, once per token. The two are so different that some systems run them on separate groups of GPUs3637.

The maths adding up the wait, step by step
In words

The wait for the first word is a sum: the trip there, the checks, the line, the reading, any thinking, writing one token, and the trip back. The whole reply then adds one more token's time for every remaining token.

With your numbers

40 ms (internet) + 12 ms (gateway) + 25 ms (safety) + – (line) + 6 ms (router and tokens)
+ – (reading) + – (thinking) + – (first token) + 40 ms (trip back)
= – until the first word
Writing pace: one token per – = – tokens a second, about – words a second

In symbols
Tfirst = ttrip + tcheck + W + tread + nthink·ttok + ttok
W = s · ρ / (1 − ρ)    tread ≈ 30 ms + nnew × 0.08 ms
Tall = Tfirst + (nout − 1) · ttok

Tfirst time until the first word · ttrip the internet both ways (80 ms) · tcheck gateway, safety check, router and tokenizing (43 ms) · W average wait in line · s time for one place to free up (60 ms) · ρ how busy the service is, from 0 to 1 · tread reading time · nnew tokens not already cached · nthink hidden thinking tokens (300 or 0) · ttok time to write one token (18–26 ms, slower when busier) · Tall time for the whole reply · nout tokens in the reply. All the constants are illustrative.

Check yourself

Your prompt is 3,000 tokens long and the reply is 50 tokens. Ignoring the line, what mostly decides the wait before the first word?

The first word can't appear until the whole prompt has been read. The other 49 reply tokens come after the first one, and encryption is cheap.

A service goes from 50% busy to 95% busy. What happens to the average wait in line?

The wait grows like ρ/(1 − ρ): 1 at 50% busy, 19 at 95%. That's why providers keep spare capacity.
