Chapter 9 · Serving millions

What does one AI reply actually cost?

A GPU costs the same per hour whether it is busy or idle. So the price of a reply depends less on the reply than on how full the GPUs are kept. Run them too empty and you pay for nothing; run them too full and people wait. This chapter gives you a fleet and a day of traffic.

About 15 minutes · one simulation · you'll learn what a reply costs and why, why waiting shoots up as GPUs fill, why providers sell spare time cheaply, and why output, input and cached tokens are priced differently.

What's going on

A provider pays for GPUs by the hour, used or not. One GPU here can finish about 11,500 replies an hour if every seat is always full, so at $3 an hour a reply costs about $0.26 per thousand, a fortieth of a cent. That is the floor. So why not run every GPU completely full? Because people arrive at random. When nearly every seat is taken, a new arrival often finds none free and has to wait for one, and those waits pile up. Waiting stays near zero until GPUs are about 85% full, then climbs steeply: see the curve in the simulation. Right now your seats are – full.

Traffic also moves. It rises through the day, falls at night, and jumps when something happens. Starting a new GPU takes minutes, so a fleet needs spare room for surprises. The trick is not to waste that room: providers fill it with batch jobs, work that isn't urgent and can step aside the moment a chat needs the seat.

How it works headroom, batch work and token prices

Queueing. Waiting at a busy service follows the maths of queues. With many seats, a GPU can run quite full before anyone waits, because the chance that all 64 are busy at once stays tiny. Past about 85% that chance grows fast, and the average wait grows roughly as 1 ÷ (1 − how full), which heads to infinity as the GPU approaches 100%. That's why services keep headroom and why an autoscaler's target is a trade-off between cost and waiting.

Selling spare time. Requests that can wait an hour, like summarising a pile of documents overnight, are a perfect fit for spare seats. Anthropic, for example, charges half price for requests sent as a batch; they finish within 24 hours, and usually in under one902. The provider gets paid for time that would otherwise be wasted, and the customer gets a discount for not being in a hurry.

Why tokens are priced differently. The price list follows the GPU time each kind of token uses. Reading your prompt (input) happens in one big parallel pass, so each input token is a small slice of busy maths. Writing (output) takes a whole step per token, and each step is mostly the memory read from the last chapter, so output tokens hold a seat far longer. That's why output usually costs several times more than input: five times, on Anthropic's price list904. Cached input is cheaper still. If the start of your prompt matches one the server saw recently, its notes are already stored (chapter 7), so they don't need working out again; reading them from the cache is billed at a tenth of the normal input price or less904.

Energy. Google measured the median text prompt to its Gemini assistant at 0.24 watt-hours, counting the whole datacenter, including the host computers, idle spare machines and the building's overheads such as cooling901. The simulation counts the GPU alone for a smaller model, so its figure is lower. The same lesson holds: idle machines still use power, so a fuller fleet uses less energy per reply.

The maths cost per reply and waiting
In words

A reply costs the GPU's hourly price divided by how many replies the GPU finishes in an hour. That number is its seats, times how many replies each seat finishes an hour, times how full the seats are kept. Waiting depends on how full they are too: the closer to full, the longer the wait.

In symbols
cost = P · R ÷ (3,600 · S · ρ)
W ≈ c · R ÷ (S · (1 − ρ))

cost dollars per reply · P price per GPU-hour ($3) · R seconds a reply holds a seat (20) · S seats per GPU (64) · ρ ("rho") how full the seats are, from 0 to 1 · W average wait · c the chance that every seat is taken when someone arrives (the "Erlang C" formula, which grows quickly as ρ nears 1). Cost falls as 1 ÷ ρ, but the wait has (1 − ρ) underneath, so it explodes as ρ approaches 1.
