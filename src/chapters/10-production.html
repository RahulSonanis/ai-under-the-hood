What happens when something goes wrong in production?

At Google, most incidents begin with a change someone made on purpose1001. In August 2025, three separate bugs made some replies from Claude, Anthropic's AI assistant, quietly worse for weeks. The servers kept answering; some answers were just worse. This chapter puts you in charge of shipping a new version without letting that happen.

You ship a new version

People's requests (teal) reach a router, which sends each one to a server. Servers turn amber when they run the new version. The dashboard panel compares the new version (amber lines) with the old one (grey) on four measures. The new version has a hidden fault: try shipping it to everyone at once, then try small steps. Your goal is to get the new version to all traffic while letting no more than 20 garbled replies reach people. Numbers are illustrative and time is sped up.

What's going on

Running a model for millions of people means changing it all the time: new versions, new settings, faster code. Each change is a risk. Google's reliability engineers report that most of their incidents are triggered by pushing new code or new settings1001. So the new version first goes to a small slice of traffic, the canary (named after the birds miners carried to warn of bad air). Its numbers are compared with the old version's at the same moment, on the same kind of traffic.

The hard part is choosing what to watch. Error counts and speed catch crashes and slowdowns. A reply full of wrong characters is neither: it arrives on time and counts as a success. Only something that reads replies, an automatic quality check, sees it quickly. Right now 0% of traffic is on the new version and 0 garbled replies have reached people, out of the 20 you're allowed.

The slice also has to be the right shape. The router weights servers, so a 1% slice can be a single server. In the simulation, the first 1% and 5% land only on one chip A server, so a fault on chip C can't appear there; 25% is the first slice that reaches chip C. That is a weakness of this "in steps" plan: a better first slice would hold one server of each chip type, so a fault on any of them shows up while only a few people are exposed. Real providers run the same model on several kinds of chip, and a bug can live on one of them.

When a watched line crosses its limit, the router sends everyone back to the old version: a rollback. It doesn't fix anything. It stops the damage and buys engineers time to find the cause. Rollback is fast only because the old version is kept running and warm alongside the new one (often called blue/green); loading a model's weights onto fresh servers takes minutes.

Teams write their targets down as service-level objectives (SLOs), such as "99.9% of replies succeed". The gap between the target and perfect is an error budget: while it lasts, releases go ahead; once it is spent, releases stop1002. Your 20 garbled replies are a budget of that kind. When a watched signal moves past its target, including the quality signal, an alert pages (calls) the engineer on duty, day or night.

How it works canaries, the on-call dashboard and a real incident

Canary releases. Google's reliability engineers define canarying as "a partial and time-limited deployment of a change in a service and its evaluation". They advise comparing the canary with a control group running the old version, and picking a handful of measures that reflect what users experience, not internal ones like processor load1001. The simulation's "in steps" plan (1%, 5%, 25%, 50%, 100%, pausing at each step) is one common shape.

What's on a real dashboard. An engineer on call watches a handful of charts, and each one is something this course has shown you. Every chart is split by model version and chip type, so a canary can be compared with the old version:

  • Time to first token, the latency people feel most, including its slowest 1%, not just the average: reading the prompt plus time in the line (chapter 1, chapter 9).
  • Tokens per second for each person once writing starts. It falls as batches fill and chats grow long (chapter 8).
  • Queue length: people waiting for a seat. It shoots up as the seats near full (chapter 9).
  • KV memory use: how full GPU memory is with conversations' notes, and how many were paused for lack of room (chapter 7).
  • Errors and quality flags: failed requests and "too many requests" refusals (chapter 1), plus the automatic quality check and people's thumbs-down (this chapter).
  • GPU utilisation: the share of seats in use, against the autoscaler's target (chapter 9).
  • Power and temperature: a cooling problem first shows as chips slowing themselves down (chapter 11).
  • Cost per 1,000 replies, and the prompt-cache hit rate that pulls it down (chapter 9, chapter 16).
  • Safety detector hits: jailbreak and prompt-injection attempts caught on the way in or out (chapter 17).

Google's engineers advise judging a service by a few measures that reflect what people experience1001, so waits, errors and quality are the ones that page someone at night. The rest help explain why.

What happened in 2025. Anthropic, the company that makes Claude, published a postmortem (a write-up of what went wrong and why) of three bugs from August and September 202530. One sent some requests to servers set up for a different context length (how much text one request can hold); in the worst hour it touched 16% of requests to one model. A second, a misconfiguration on some of its servers that use TPUs (Google-made AI chips), made the model sometimes pick tokens that should almost never appear, such as Thai or Chinese characters in English replies. A third was a bug in the compiler (the tool that turns the model's code into instructions for the chip) on the same chips that sometimes dropped the most likely next token. Anthropic serves Claude on three chip families, and the bugs showed up differently on each, which made reports confusing.

Why it was hard to see. Anthropic says its usual changes go to small canary groups first, yet "the evaluations we ran simply didn't capture the degradation users were reporting", partly because the model often recovers from a single bad token. Privacy rules also limit engineers' access to conversations people haven't sent as feedback. Its fixes match the lesson of the simulation: more sensitive quality checks, and running them continuously on live production traffic, not just before release30.

The maths how many people a bug reaches
In words

The bad replies that reach people are: replies per second, times the share of traffic on the new version, times how often the new version gets a reply wrong, times how many seconds pass before someone rolls back.

With your numbers

On chip C the new version garbles 1 in 25 replies. Once a third of the new servers are chip C, that is about 1 in 75 of all the new version's replies; until the slice reaches a chip C server, it is 0.
Your last rollout: 400 replies a second × 25% × 1/75 × 10 s before rollback ≈ 13 garbled replies. The simulation counted nothing yet.
Everyone at once for the same time: 400 × 100% × 1/75 × 10 s ≈ 53

In symbols
B = R · a · f · T

B bad replies that reach people · R replies per second across the whole service · a share of traffic on the new version · f share of the new version's replies that are bad (0 until the slice reaches the faulty chip) · T seconds until rollback. A canary shrinks a; watching the right signal shrinks T. If nothing watches the right signal, T keeps growing and so does B.

Canary first Carry this forward

New versions go to a small slice of traffic first, watched closely against the old version, before reaching everyone; the watching has to include the quality of the replies, not just errors and speed.

Check yourself

A new version garbles some replies, yet the error-rate chart stays flat. Why?

Error counters see crashes and timeouts. Bad content needs a check that actually reads replies.

Why should the first small slice of traffic include every kind of hardware the service runs on?

A canary only tests what it runs on. If it skips a chip type, it can pass while that chip's bug waits for the full rollout.
