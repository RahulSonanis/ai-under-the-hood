Chapter 3 · How a model answers

How does it pick the next word?

A language model never knows the next word. It has a hunch about every word it knows, and then it rolls a dice. In this chapter a real model, small enough to run in your browser, writes in front of you while you rig the dice.

About 14 minutes · one simulation · you'll learn what the model actually outputs, what temperature, top-k and top-p do, and how "surprise" (the loss) measures how good a model is.

What's going on

At every step the model looks at the text so far and gives every word it knows a probability, a chance between 0 and 100%. Those are the bars. Then a random number picks one word, with each word's chance equal to its slice of the strip. The chosen word is added to the text and the whole thing repeats. That's all "writing" is: predict, pick, append, again. Words still in the running right now: 0.

The dice matter. With temperature 0 the top word always wins, and the model loops. Raising the temperature flattens the odds so less likely words get a turn; too high and it writes word pairs it never saw (0 in its last 40 words). Top-k and top-p fix that from the other side: they throw away the long tail of unlikely words before the roll. Chatbots use these same knobs, usually set by the company running them.

When the model reads a sentence instead of writing, we can measure how surprised it is by each true word: a word it gave 90% costs about 0.1, a word it gave 1% costs about 4.6. The average surprise is called the loss. On sentences it studied, your model's loss is –; on new sentences it is –. Training a model means nudging it, again and again, so that this number goes down.

How it works logits, samplers and loss

Your tiny model. It is a genuine language model, just a very small one. It learned from 49 short sentences written for this course, 155 different words in all, by counting which word follows each pair of words. When it has never seen a pair, it falls back to what follows the last word alone, and then to how common each word is. That fallback is where the "word pairs it never saw" come from. A large model replaces counting with a neural network that can generalise, and its vocabulary is far larger: Meta's open Llama 3 models choose among about 128,000 tokens at every step9. The sampling step works the same way.

Logits and softmax. A real model's last layer outputs one score, called a logit, for every token in its vocabulary. A function called softmax turns those scores into probabilities that add up to 100%. Temperature divides every score before the softmax. A component called the sampler then applies top-k (keep the k best), top-p, also called nucleus sampling (keep the smallest set of top tokens whose chances reach p), and draws one39. Sampling is the only random step: at temperature 0 the same prompt gives the same reply, apart from tiny differences in how a server adds up numbers.

Loss. The surprise −ln p of the true next token, averaged over all the text, is the cross-entropy loss. It is the one number every training run watches go down, and lower loss on held-out text (text the model never trained on) generally tracks a more capable model1. Your model's loss on its own sentences is low because it has nearly memorised them. That's why researchers always measure on text the model has not seen.

Why models make things up. Next-word prediction rewards text that sounds right. Usually that is also true. When the model hasn't learned a fact, a fluent guess can still look likely, and the model doesn't notice. Your tiny model shows the root cause: at temperature 1 it happily writes grammatical sentences that were never in its training text.

The maths temperature and surprise, step by step
In words

Each word's chance comes from a score. Temperature divides every score before the scores are turned into chances, which stretches or squashes the gaps between words. Surprise is minus the natural log of the chance the model gave the true word.

pᵢ chance of word i · zᵢ its score (logit) · T temperature · Σⱼ add up over every word j in the vocabulary, so the chances total 1 · exp the exponential function, which makes every score positive · L the loss · N number of words read · xₜ the true word at position t · ln natural logarithm. As T shrinks to 0 the top word takes everything; at T = 1 the model's own odds are untouched; as T grows every word tends to the same chance.

Check yourself

You set the temperature to 0. What happens?

Low temperature sharpens the odds; at 0 the top word always wins. That's called greedy decoding, and it tends to repeat itself.

A model gave the true next word a probability of 1%. How much does that add to its loss?

Loss is the surprise −ln p of the true word. A 90% guess costs about 0.1; a 1% guess costs about 4.6.
