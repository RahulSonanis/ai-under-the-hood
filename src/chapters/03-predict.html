How does a model pick the next word?

Short answer

It gives every possible next token a probability, then a dice roll weighted by those probabilities picks one. The chosen token is added to the text and the whole thing repeats.

Probabilities, not answers

The model never just "knows" the next word. It has a hunch for every word, like "I'm pretty sure it's blue, but it might be grey". Then it picks one.

For every position, the model outputs a score for every token in its vocabulary. Those scores are turned into probabilities that add up to 100%. Picking the top one every time sounds safe but tends to be dull and repetitive; picking at random in proportion to the probabilities gives more natural text.

The final layer produces one logit per vocabulary entry (about 128k for Llama 3). A softmax converts logits to probabilities, and a sampler picks the token. Common controls: temperature (sharpen or flatten), top-k (keep k best), top-p or nucleus sampling (keep the smallest set with total probability ≥ p)39. This small piece of code matters in production: one of the bugs in Anthropic's 2025 postmortem was an approximate top-k operation, miscompiled on TPUs, that sometimes dropped the most likely token30.

With logits zᵢ and temperature T:

pᵢ = exp(zᵢ / T) / Σⱼ exp(zⱼ / T)

As T → 0 this approaches argmax (greedy decoding); T = 1 leaves the model's distribution unchanged; T → ∞ approaches uniform. Top-p keeps the smallest set S with Σ_{i∈S} pᵢ ≥ p and renormalises. Sampling is the only random step: with greedy decoding and exact arithmetic the output is deterministic, though in practice batching and floating-point order can still cause tiny differences.

Surprise is the score

If the right word was one the model thought was likely, it gets a small "oops". If it thought the right word was very unlikely, it gets a big "oops". Learning means making fewer big oopses.

We measure surprise as how unlikely the model thought the true word was. Average that over trillions of words and you get the loss, the single number every training run watches go down.

The loss is cross-entropy: the average of −log p(true token). Perplexity, e^loss, reads as "how many equally likely choices the model is torn between". A loss of 2.0 nats means perplexity of about 7.4. Lower loss on held-out text generally tracks better downstream ability, though not perfectly1.

L(θ) = −(1/T) Σₜ log p_θ(xₜ | x_{<t}) , PPL = exp(L)

Minimising L is equivalent to minimising the KL divergence from the data distribution to the model, up to the data's own entropy, which no model can beat.

Why do models sometimes make things up?

The model is trying to say something that sounds right. Usually that's also true. Sometimes it isn't, and the model doesn't notice.

Next-word prediction rewards plausible text. If the model hasn't learned a fact, a fluent guess can still score well on "sounds right". Training it to say "I'm not sure", and giving it tools like search, reduces this but doesn't remove it.

Mitigations include post-training that rewards calibrated answers and abstaining, retrieval so answers can cite sources, and evaluations that measure factual accuracy. Your tiny model above shows the root cause clearly: it happily continues into grammatical sentences that were never in its training text.

Check yourself

You set temperature to 0. What happens?

Low temperature sharpens the distribution; at 0 it becomes greedy decoding.

A model gave the true next word a probability of 1%. Is that a big or small contribution to its loss?

Loss is the surprise −log p of the true token. A 90% guess costs about 0.1; a 1% guess costs about 4.6.
