Where does the knowledge come from?

Short answer

Mostly from public web pages, plus code, books, papers and increasingly text written by other models: 15 to 40 trillion tokens for recent large models96566. Most of the raw web is thrown away; cleaning and choosing the mix is one of the most important jobs in building a model.

How much text, and from where?

The model reads more than any person ever could: a huge library made of web pages, computer code, books and articles. Reading all of that is how it learns words, facts and how ideas fit together.

Meta trained Llama 3 on about 15 trillion tokens, about eight times what the Llama 3 paper counts for Llama 2 (1.8 trillion)9. DeepSeek-V3 used 14.8 trillion10, and by 2025 Qwen3 reported 36 trillion65. At roughly 0.75 words per token, 15 trillion tokens is over ten trillion words: a reader going non-stop at 250 words a minute would need over 80,000 years.

The backbone is usually Common Crawl, a public archive of web snapshots. Hugging Face's FineWeb processed 96 snapshots into a 15T-token English dataset: extract text from HTML, identify language, apply quality heuristics, deduplicate per snapshot with MinHash, anonymise email and IP addresses3. Code comes from public repositories, maths from filtered web pages, and many teams add synthetic data generated by stronger models. The final mixture (how much web vs code vs maths vs other languages) is tuned with small experiments.

MinHash estimates the Jaccard similarity J(A, B) = |A∩B| / |A∪B| of two documents' shingle sets: for a random hash h, Pr[min h(A) = min h(B)] = J(A, B). With k hash functions the estimate is the fraction of matching minima, with standard error √(J(1−J)/k). Locality-sensitive hashing groups the k minima into b bands of r rows so that likely duplicates collide in at least one band, avoiding all-pairs comparison.

Why "best" data isn't always best

If you only read very hard science papers, you'd learn lots of facts but talk in a strange way. A good reading diet is varied.

Teams can't just pick what sounds smartest. Hugging Face found that scientific papers from arXiv, full of knowledge, can actually hurt smaller models, because their narrow academic style is unlike the broad text models learn best from1. Mixtures are decided by running experiments, not by intuition.

Teams run ablations: train small models on alternative mixtures and compare benchmark scores. SmolLM3's team spent more GPU time on ablations and debugging than half their main run1. Training often happens in stages, with higher-quality code, maths and long documents emphasised toward the end ("annealing" or mid-training).

Rights, privacy and contamination

Not everything on the internet should be used. Some owners say no, some pages contain private details, and some pages are the answers to the test.

Common practices include respecting robots.txt opt-outs, removing personal information, and removing known benchmark questions so test scores stay honest. Copyright and consent for training data are being actively argued in courts and parliaments, and the rules differ between countries.

Benchmark contamination happens when test items leak into training data, inflating scores. Labs decontaminate by n-gram matching against evaluation sets and increasingly favour fresh, held-out or private evaluations (chapter 13).

Packing documents into training sequences Engineer tool

Documents are concatenated and cut into fixed-length sequences. Over 80% of documents in several popular datasets are under 2k tokens1, so each sequence mixes unrelated texts. Intra-document masking stops tokens attending across documents; Llama 3 and SmolLM3 used it, mainly for long-context training91. Check yourself

Why remove near-duplicate pages, not just exact copies?

The same article with a different footer is effectively one document. Training on it many times adds little and raises memorisation.

How do teams usually decide the data mixture?

Intuition about data quality is often wrong, so mixtures are chosen by ablation experiments.
